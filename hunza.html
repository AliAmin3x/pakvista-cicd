Hunza Valley

A hidden paradise nestled among the peaks of the Karakoram Range.

About Hunza Valley

Hunza Valley is one of Pakistan's most celebrated destinations, located in the Gilgit-Baltistan region at an altitude of approximately 2,438 metres above sea level. Surrounded by towering peaks of the Karakoram Range — including Rakaposhi, Ultar Sar, and Bojahagur Duanasir II — the valley is renowned for its dramatic scenery, terraced apricot and cherry orchards, and the legendary longevity of its people.

The valley was once part of the ancient Silk Road trading routes, and its people — the Burusho — speak the unique Burushaski language, unrelated to any other known language family. Today, Hunza is one of the most visited destinations in Pakistan, attracting trekkers, photographers, and culture enthusiasts from around the world. The Karakoram Highway (KKH), one of the highest paved international roads in the world, provides access to the valley.

Best Time to Visit

April to October — spring brings blossoming apricot trees; summer is ideal for trekking and high-altitude exploration.

Rakaposhi Viewpoint

Rakaposhi, at 7,788 metres, is the 27th highest mountain in the world and the dominant peak visible from the Hunza Valley floor. What makes Rakaposhi exceptional is not just its height but the sheer vertical rise — from the Hunza River at roughly 1,500 metres to the summit, the mountain rises nearly 6,300 metres in horizontal distance of only about 16 kilometres, one of the greatest base-to-summit rises of any mountain on Earth.

The most accessible viewpoint is located near the village of Minapin, where a dedicated viewpoint platform offers an unobstructed view of the entire south face of the mountain. On clear mornings, the peak is reflected in the still waters of the Hunza River far below. The Rakaposhi Base Camp trek (moderate difficulty, 2–3 days) takes trekkers through alpine meadows to an elevation of approximately 3,500 metres, with breathtaking close-up views of glaciers and the surrounding peaks.

Attabad Lake

Attabad Lake is one of the most visually striking lakes in all of Pakistan, famous for its intense turquoise colour that glows brilliantly against the grey and brown of the surrounding Karakoram mountains. The lake was formed in January 2010 when a massive landslide near the village of Attabad blocked the Hunza River, creating a lake that grew to 21 kilometres in length over the following months.

The landslide displaced thousands of local residents and submerged several villages along with a stretch of the original Karakoram Highway. The Pakistan government later built tunnels through the mountains to restore the KKH, which now pass beneath the lake. Today, Attabad Lake is a major tourist attraction. Visitors can take boat rides across its shimmering waters, go kayaking, or simply sit along the shoreline and take in the surreal landscape. The contrast between the vivid blue-green water and the stark rocky mountains makes it one of the most photographed locations in Pakistan.

Baltit Fort

Baltit Fort is a historic fortress perched dramatically above the town of Karimabad in the Hunza Valley. The fort is believed to be over 700 years old and served as the residence of the Mirs (rulers) of the ancient Hunza State for centuries. The architecture of the fort shows strong Tibetan and Balti influences — particularly in its wood-carved balconies, sloping roofs, and mud-brick walls — reflecting the cultural exchanges that occurred along the Silk Road.

The fort was abandoned in the 1940s when the last Mir moved to a new palace at the base of the hill. Over the following decades it fell into disrepair, but was extensively restored between 1990 and 1996 with support from the Aga Khan Trust for Culture. Today it is a museum open to visitors and offers one of the finest viewpoints in all of Hunza — from its upper terraces, the Hunza Valley, Attabad Lake, and the ring of surrounding peaks are all visible. It is a UNESCO Asia-Pacific Heritage Award winner.
